1.1 Cognition in the Brain


A central question of cognitive science is how cognition is reflected in the structure and function of the brain. Historically, the answer developed from speculative faculty psychology over cytoarchitectonic maps to modern digital brain atlases.

### Phrenology

Phrenology, from grk. *phren* (mind) and *logos* (knowledge), was a predecessor of cognitive science founded by the German physician Franz Joseph Gall at the end of the 18th century. It described the mind based on assumptions about the anatomical structure of the brain:

- the brain is the organ of the mind,
- human mental power consists of independent faculties located at specific brain regions,
- the size of a brain region indicates how much the faculty contributes to a person's character,
- the sizes of brain regions correspond closely to the shape of the skull.

Phrenology is not a science, since the assignment of "organs" to brain regions is completely arbitrary. Three of its hypotheses remain valid in modern cognitive neuroscience: the brain as center of thought, modular organization into anatomical regions, and functional specialization of individual regions.

### Anatomical architecture

| Year | Contribution |
| ---- | ------------ |
| 1907 | Korbinian Brodmann divides the cerebral cortex into 52 regions based on cytoarchitectonic properties, today known as the Brodman areas, and claims that individual regions correspond to specific functions |
| 2013 | Amunts et al. build an ultrahigh-resolution 3D human brain model |

The 3D model is created by cutting the brain into 7400 histological sections of 20 µm thickness, staining every section to identify cell bodies, digitizing them at 13,000 by 11,000 pixels, removing scan artifacts and aligning all slices into a 3D reconstruction.

### Motor and sensory regions of the cerebral cortex

Primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus) and primary sensory cortex (postcentral gyrus), the somatic motor association area (premotor cortex) and the somatic sensory association area, prefrontal cortex, auditory cortex and auditory association area, visual cortex and visual association area, plus the two speech regions: Broca's area (production of speech) and Wernicke's area (understanding of speech).
