1.3.1 Cognitivist Systems


Cognitivist cognitive modeling rests on the hypothesis that cognition is a form of computation and that cognitive functions can be modeled as working computer programs.

### Cognitivism and AI

- Cognitivism started as a sister discipline of Artificial Intelligence during a summer school at Dartmouth College in 1956.
- Central hypothesis: cognition is achieved by operations on symbols that have a one-to-one correspondence to real-world actions and objects.
- External information from the environment is gathered and filtered by perceptual processes.
- Over time the focus of AI shifted to purely computational techniques; AI with a focus on cognition is today called Artificial General Intelligence.

### Philosophy of mind and the mind-body problem

Cognitivism postulates that all cognitive processes can be explained mechanistically through some form of computation. The philosophy of mind challenges this simplifying view by asking about the basic nature of mental phenomena (thought, feeling, perception, consciousness, sensory experience), whether they can be described solely by chemical or physical processes or can exist independently of them, and how subjective conscious experiences (qualia) are explained. The core question is the relation between the mind (mental world) and the body (physical world).

| School | Claim |
| ------ | ----- |
| Substance dualism (Cartesian dualism) | René Descartes postulated that mind and body are two different substances; their separation makes the soul immortal and enables free will. He falsely assumed that the pineal gland interfaces the mind with the physical world |
| Monism (physicalism) | There is only one substance, so mental states are physical states. This implies that individuals sharing a mental property share a corresponding physical property; nonreductive physicalism therefore postulates that mental properties are not identical to physical ones despite the single substance |

### The computational theory of mind

The CTM is the theoretical basis of cognitivism: it "holds that the mind is a digital computer: a discrete-state device that stores symbolic representations and manipulates them according to syntactic rules; that thoughts are mental representations, more specifically symbolic representations in a language of thought; and that mental processes are causal sequences driven by the syntactic, but not by the semantic, properties of symbols" (MIT Encyclopedia of the Cognitive Sciences).

Computers are viewed as a proof that purely syntactic operations can express the causality in the semantics of cognitive processes, so cognition can be implemented in a purely physical system.

### Physical Symbol Systems

The concept of Physical Symbol Systems (PSS) by Newell and Simon lays the theoretical foundations for the emergence of general intelligence from symbolic operations:

- a PSS consists of physical patterns called symbols which form expressions that are created, modified, copied and destroyed by processes,
- an expression designates an object iff the system can affect it or is affected by it,
- the system can interpret an expression iff it designates a process the system can carry out,
- symbols and processes are defined recursively: symbols can designate symbols, processes can produce processes (→ development),
- problem solving is done by searching.

| Hypothesis (Newell and Simon, 1975) | Statement | Consequence |
| ----------------------------------- | --------- | ----------- |
| Physical Symbol System Hypothesis | A physical symbol system has the necessary and sufficient means for general intelligent action | Every cognitive system is a physical symbol system |
| Heuristic Search Hypothesis | Solutions are represented as symbol structures; a PSS exercises its intelligence in problem-solving by search, generating and progressively modifying symbol structures until it produces a solution structure | Cognition is reflected in an intelligent search strategy that finds a solution without exhaustive brute-force search |

### Rationality

Rationality means that an agent acts in a sensible and purposeful way to achieve its goals, always using as much of its knowledge as possible to guide its behavior.

| View | Statement |
| ---- | --------- |
| Optimal rationality | An ideal agent, "given its belief-desire system, optimizes its choices"; not feasible in reality |
| Bounded rationality | "Rationality as exhibited by decision makers of limited abilities" |

Rationality depends on the success criterion, the agent's prior knowledge of the environment, the actions it can perform, and past percepts.
