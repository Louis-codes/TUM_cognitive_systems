5.3 Model Selection and Generalization


The model $\mathcal{M}$ of a machine learning system encodes, stores and retrieves the outcome of the learning process. Adapting $\mathcal{M}$ with a learning algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ to find an optimal model corresponds to a search in a hypothesis space $\mathcal{H}$:

$$\mathcal{M}^* = \arg\min_{\mathcal{M} \in \mathcal{H}} \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M})$$

- In most practical applications the global minimum cannot be found, so learning algorithms search for local optima.
- $\mathcal{H}$ determines which aspects of the data are captured (simple statistics, probability distributions) and how they are represented (implicit or human-readable).
- Hypothesis spaces support the incorporation of a priori knowledge to different degrees (for example neural networks vs. logic).

### Common hypothesis spaces

Decision trees, polynomials, logic, neural networks, Bayesian networks, Markov models.

### Ensemble methods and boosting

Ensemble methods extend hypothesis spaces by combining hypotheses $h_1, \dots, h_n \in \mathcal{H}$ into a new hypothesis $h^* \in \mathcal{H}^n$. The guiding question is whether a **strong learner** can be built from several **weak learners** with accuracies just above chance.

**Boosting** constructs such an ensemble incrementally:

- every training sample $s_i \in \mathcal{S}$ is assigned a weight $w_i$, initially all equal,
- weights of incorrectly learned samples are increased,
- training of new hypotheses focuses on samples with high weights.

Example: AdaBoost with 300 boosted decision trees approximates a function far better than a single regression tree.

### Generative and discriminative models

Probabilistic models can either discriminate data drawn from the distribution $\mathcal{D}$ that generates $\mathcal{S}$, or model $\mathcal{D}$ directly. Samples $(x,y)$ are distributed according to $P(x,y)$.

| Model type | Basis | Prediction |
| ---------- | ----- | ---------- |
| Discriminative | Posterior probabilities $P(y \mid x)$ | Directly computed |
| Generative | Prior probabilities $P(x \mid y)$ | Via Bayes' theorem $P(y \mid x) = \frac{P(x \mid y)P(y)}{P(x)}$ |

Generative models are compact representations of the training data with considerably fewer parameters than $\mathcal{S}$. Examples: generative modeling of faces, and Generative Query Networks that learn internal scene representations in a representation network and predict novel camera views through a generation network.
