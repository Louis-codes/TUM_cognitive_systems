2.1 Cognitive Architectures


A cognitive architecture is the specification of a cognitive system that defines all of its components and the relations between them. It answers the question of how a cognitive system is actually implemented.

**Definition 3.1**: a cognitive architecture is the overall, essential structure and process of a broadly-scoped domain-generic computational cognitive model, used for a broad, multiple-level, multi-domain analysis of cognition and behavior. In particular, it deals with componential processes of cognition in a structurally and mechanistically well defined way. Its function is to provide an essential framework to facilitate more detailed modeling and understanding of various components and processes of the mind (adapted from Sun, 2004).

The word architecture (lat. *architectura*) itself denotes a unifying or coherent form or structure, a method or style of building, and the manner in which the components of a computer system are organized and integrated.

### Unified theory of cognition

The design of cognitive architectures was originally motivated in cognitivist cognitive science by Newell's concept of a unified theory of cognition:

- it addresses a "broad range of cognitive issues, such as attention, memory, problem solving, decision making, and learning" (Vernon, 2014),
- the issues are covered "from several aspects including psychology, neuroscience, and computer science" (Vernon, 2014),
- all cognitivist cognitive architectures are unified theories of cognition.

The instantiation of a cognitive architecture with a specific body of knowledge is called a **cognitive model**.

### Goals

| Goal | Characteristics |
| ---- | --------------- |
| Modelling of the human mind | Advance the understanding of cognition through concise, computer-executable descriptions of cognitive processes; capture and reproduce experimental data in models that are not optimal but realistic; focus on generality and robustness |
| Construction of artificial cognitive systems | Design artificial systems as capable as living creatures; "brute-force" solutions (search, optimization, big data); focus on domain-specific and optimal methods |

### Advantages

- Cognitive architectures "shed new light on cognition and therefore they are useful tools for advancing the understanding of human cognition" (Sun, 2007).
- They are generic and can be applied in different domains (frameworks for cognitive systems, as opposed to expert systems).
- They are unified models that coherently integrate many different aspects of cognition.
- They are executable implementations of theories about cognition that explicitly state all underlying assumptions and hypotheses.
- They are "deep explanations" (Sun, 2007) of cognitive processing that go beyond the task-level.

### Levels of modeling

After Sun (2007), models address four levels; the cognitive architecture sits at the componential level, constrained from below by the physiological level:

| Level | Content |
| ----- | ------- |
| Sociological | Interaction between agents |
| Psychological | Cognition in individual agents |
| Componential | Cognitive architecture |
| Physiological | Biological implementation (imposes computational constraints) |
