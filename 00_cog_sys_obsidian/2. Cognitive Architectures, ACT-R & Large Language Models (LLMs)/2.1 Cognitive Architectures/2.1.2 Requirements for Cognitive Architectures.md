2.1.2 Requirements for Cognitive Architectures


A cognitive architecture is a complex coherent model that must support a broad spectrum of cognitive functions: "perception, categorization, representation, different types of memory, decision-making, reasoning, planning, problem-solving, meta-cognition, communication, action control and execution, different types of learning, motivational processes, goal representation, etc." (Sun, 2007). All of these functions are interdependent, their interaction is dynamic and task-dependent, and the architecture must capture these dynamics.

### Design considerations

| Consideration | Tradeoff |
| ------------- | -------- |
| Scope (compactness and comprehensiveness) | Architectures are generic and thus minimalistic models, so modeling all relevant cognitive functions explicitly is not desirable. The designer decides which functions are an integral part of the architecture and which can be implemented later within its framework (derived capabilities). Components do not necessarily capture an agent's phylogeny (Sun, 2004) |
| Generality (simplicity and degree of realism) | Modeling too many specific details makes the architecture less general, while a too general formulation makes it under-constrained and meaningless (Sun, 2007) |

### Desirable characteristics

| Group | Requirements |
| ----- | ------------ |
| Realism | **Ecological realism**: capability to engage in everyday activities of the natural environment. **Bio-evolutionary realism**: a model of human intelligence should be reducible to a model of animal intelligence. **Cognitive realism**: capture all essential characteristics of human cognition with respect to psychology, philosophy and neuroscience. **Eclecticism**: incorporation of results from prior research |
| Behavioral characteristics | **Reactivity**: quick reactions through immediate and fixed responses without elaborate processing. **Sequentiality**: activities executed step by step over a timespan. **Routineness**: learning, adaption and execution of reoccurring action patterns, for example by trial and error |
| Cognitive characteristics | **Explicit processes**: accessible and precise. **Implicit processes**: inaccessible and imprecise. **Synergistic interaction**: implicit and explicit processes complement and supplement each other. **Learning**: top-down (explicit first, implicit later) and bottom-up (implicit first, explicit later). **Modularity**: some faculties are specialized and separated through functional or physical encapsulation |
| Development | **Value systems and motives**: guidance during action selection and motivational drive for exploration. **Physical embodiment**: the body enables acting in the world and perceiving the effects of actions. **Sensorimotor contingencies**: store the relations between actions and resulting sensory feedback. **Perception**: capacity for unsupervised perceptual categorization. **Constitutive autonomy**: autonomous operation of developmental processes |
